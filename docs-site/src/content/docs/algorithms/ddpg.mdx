**Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient** is the ancestor of the off-policy
continuous-control family. A deterministic actor, a single critic, and Gaussian
noise for exploration.

## The idea

For discrete actions, [DQN](/algorithms/dqn/) picks the best action by taking a
`max` over the network's outputs. With continuous actions that maximisation is
itself an optimization problem — you cannot enumerate the options.

DDPG's answer: train a network to *output* the maximising action. The actor is
updated by pushing its actions toward higher critic values, backpropagating
through the critic into the policy.

```
actor loss  = −Q(s, π(s))          # maximise the critic's estimate
critic loss = (Q(s,a) − y)²        # y = r + γ·Q′(s′, π′(s′))
```

That trick — differentiate through the critic to improve the actor — is what
every algorithm in this family inherits.

### Why the chain rule is the whole algorithm

The deterministic policy gradient theorem says the objective's gradient with
respect to the actor's parameters factors through the action:

```
∇_θ J = 𝔼_s [ ∇_a Q(s, a)|_(a = π(s)) · ∇_θ π(s) ]
```

Read it right to left. The actor produces an action; the critic reports which
direction in action space increases value; the chain rule carries that direction
back into the actor's weights. The critic is not just a baseline here, as it is
in [A2C](/algorithms/a2c/) — it is the *source* of the gradient. If the critic's
action-space slope is wrong, the actor is not slowed down, it is pointed the
wrong way.

That distinction explains why this family is so much more sensitive to critic
quality than policy-gradient methods, and why every successor
([TD3](/algorithms/td3/), [SAC](/algorithms/sac/), [REDQ](/algorithms/redq/))
attacks the *critic* rather than the actor.

Exploration is external: Gaussian noise added to the actor's output, since a
deterministic policy explores nothing on its own. The original paper used
temporally correlated Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise; uncorrelated Gaussian noise was
later shown to work as well or better, and is what this implementation uses —
`noise_scale`, default `0.1`, in units of the action scale.

## Building it

```mojo
from mojo_rl.deep_agents.ddpg import DDPGAgent
from mojo_rl.deep_agents.training.blocks import UniformSampleGpuStep

var agent = DDPGAgent[
    "gpu",
    UniformSampleGpuStep[OBS_DIM, ACT_DIM, BATCH, REPLAY_CAPACITY],
    ActorNet,
    CriticNet,
](
    ctx=ctx,
    actor_lr=1e-4,      # note: lower than the critic's
    critic_lr=1e-3,
    gamma=0.99,
    tau=0.005,
    noise_scale=0.1,    # exploration σ, in units of action_scale
)
```

The actor is a deterministic network ending in `Tanh` scaled to the action
range — not a [`StochasticActor`](/algorithms/sac/), which is what SAC uses.

**The asymmetric learning rates are deliberate**, and they are the one place
DDPG's defaults differ from TD3's and SAC's `3e-4`/`3e-4`. Since the actor's
gradient is manufactured by the critic, a fast actor chasing a slow critic
compounds whatever the critic has got wrong. TD3 replaces this soft mitigation
with an explicit one — updating the actor every other step — and can then afford
equal rates.

## The problem

DDPG is brittle, and understanding why explains the two algorithms that follow.

The critic is trained on targets built from its own estimates at the actor's
chosen action. Wherever the critic happens to overestimate, the actor moves
*toward* that region — because that is where the critic says the value is high.
The next target is then computed there, reinforcing the error.

This is not noise that averages out. The `max`-like structure of the update
**selects** for optimistic error. A run can look healthy for a long time and
then collapse.

Fujimoto et al. made the mechanism precise. Write the critic's error at the
actor's action as `ε`, with mean zero. Because the actor is trained to maximise
`Q`, it is drawn to states and actions where `ε` happens to be *positive* — so
the expectation of `ε` conditioned on the actor's own choice is not zero:

```
𝔼[ Q(s, π(s)) ]  >  𝔼[ Q^π(s, π(s)) ]
```

The bias enters the target, the target trains the critic, the critic re-aims the
actor. Each of the three fixes in [TD3](/algorithms/td3/) breaks one link of
that chain: the twin-min attacks the bias itself, the delay slows the loop, and
target smoothing removes the narrow peaks the actor was exploiting.

[TD3](/algorithms/td3/) fixes this with three targeted changes;
[SAC](/algorithms/sac/) fixes it differently, with a stochastic policy and an
entropy term.

## When to use it

Honestly: to understand the family. TD3 is DDPG with the known fixes applied and
is strictly better; SAC is better again on most tasks.

DDPG remains useful as a **baseline** — if a new environment trains under TD3 but
not DDPG, that is informative about how much the stabilisation matters there.
