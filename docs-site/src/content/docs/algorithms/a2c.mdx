**Advantage Actor-Critic** is the minimal on-policy deep policy gradient: collect
a rollout, estimate advantages, take one gradient step, discard the data.

## The algorithm

```
actor loss  = −log π(a|s) · Â            + entropy bonus
critic loss = (V(s) − return)²
```

An actor and a critic; the critic supplies a baseline so the actor is updated by
the **advantage** rather than the raw return. That is the whole thing.

Subtracting the critic's `V(s)` does not change what the gradient converges to —
any function of the state alone has zero expected effect on the policy
gradient — it changes the **variance** of the estimate. That is the entire
justification for having a critic in a policy-gradient method, and it is why the
critic being merely approximately right is acceptable: a wrong baseline costs
you variance reduction, not correctness. Contrast [DDPG](/algorithms/ddpg/),
where the critic *is* the gradient and being wrong points the actor the wrong
way.

The entropy bonus is the only thing keeping the policy from collapsing early.
With no clipping and one pass per batch, a confident-and-wrong policy stops
sampling the actions that would correct it.

## Its relationship to PPO

A2C is implemented here as a **degenerate single-epoch PPO** — the same trainer
with `N_EPOCHS = 1` and no clipping.

That framing is the useful way to understand both. [PPO](/algorithms/ppo/) adds
exactly two things:

**Multiple epochs.** Reuse the same rollout for several gradient passes,
extracting far more from each batch of environment interaction.

**The clipped surrogate.** Bound how far the policy can move per update, which is
what makes reusing the data safe. Without the clip, the second and third epochs
are optimising against a policy that has already changed, and the update
diverges.

So the clip is not an optimisation detail bolted onto A2C — it is the thing that
makes epoch reuse possible, and epoch reuse is where PPO's sample efficiency
comes from.

## When to use it

**Almost never, for results.** PPO is strictly better and costs nothing extra.

A2C is worth having for two reasons:

- **As a baseline.** If PPO underperforms A2C on a task, something is wrong with the clip range, the epoch count, or the advantage normalization.
- **For understanding.** A2C is small enough to read end to end, and it makes clear what PPO's additions actually buy.

## Building it

```mojo
from mojo_rl.deep_agents.a2c import A2CAgent
```

The facade mirrors [PPO](/algorithms/ppo/)'s — actor and critic types, dimensions,
rollout length — without the epoch and clip parameters.

For discrete actions, `PPODiscreteAgent` with `N_EPOCHS=1` gives the same
behaviour through the PPO surface.

## Watch out for

**Time limits break GAE**, exactly as they do for PPO. Use `was_terminated()`
rather than `done`, or every advantage in the rollout is corrupted on a
truncation. [Pendulum](/environments/classic-control/) has no terminal state at
all, so this bites immediately there.

**High variance.** With one epoch and no clipping, A2C is sensitive to learning
rate. If it is unstable, that is the algorithm, not a bug — which is the point
of PPO.
